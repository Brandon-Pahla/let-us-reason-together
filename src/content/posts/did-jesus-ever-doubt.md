<section id="intro">
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>And about the ninth hour Jesus cried out with a loud voice, saying, <span class="wj">“Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani?”</span> that is, <span class="wj">“My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?”</span></p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2027%3A46&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 27:46</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Mark records the same cry in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2015%3A34&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 15:34</a>.</p>
<p class="note">In the scripture quotations, the words of Jesus are in red.</p>
<p>Did Jesus ever doubt? I mean here, on the cross, when He asked God why He had been forsaken.</p>
<p>This question was not academic for me. For a long time something about the cross held my faith back. Jesus healed the sick. He raised Lazarus. He told His disciples He would rise again. And I believe He is fully God. So why was His death such a big deal? Why was He afraid in the garden?</p>
<p>Stephen, while he was being stoned, saw the heavens opened and prayed for the men who were killing him (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%207%3A55-60&amp;version=NKJV">Acts 7:55–60</a>). I had always heard of martyrs who went to their deaths willingly, even gladly. Why would Jesus be afraid of what His own followers faced with peace?</p>
<p><strong>My answer is yes. Jesus doubted on the cross. But He never fell into unbelief.</strong></p>
<p>That is a big claim, so I hold myself to a standard. The argument has to stand on scripture, and it has to follow logically. The rest of this post shows the working. I'm a lay reader writing up what I learned, so read it as someone thinking it through.</p>
</section>
<section id="terms">
<h2>Two words I need first</h2>
<p><strong>Doubt</strong> is wavering, being "in two minds": wanting to believe while struggling to see how. Honest doubt moves toward God with its questions, like the father who said, "<span class="sq">Lord, I believe; help my unbelief!</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A24&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 9:24</a>).</p>
<p><strong>Unbelief</strong> is the settled refusal to trust: turning away from God and closing the door. <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%203%3A12&amp;version=NKJV">Hebrews 3:12</a> warns of "<span class="sq">an evil heart of unbelief in departing from the living God</span>".</p>
<p>The difference is direction, toward God or away from Him. The other terms, and the Greek and Hebrew words behind them, are in the <a href="#words">word list</a> at the end.</p>
</section>
<section id="sin">
<h2>Is doubt a sin on its own?</h2>
<p>Before I ask this of Jesus, I want to ask it of everyone. It matters first because of this verse:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>For we do not have a High Priest who cannot sympathize with our weaknesses, but was in all <em>points</em> tempted as <em>we are,</em> <em>yet</em> without sin.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3A15&amp;version=NKJV">Hebrews 4:15</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>He was tempted as we are, yet without sin. So if doubt is itself sin, the question about Jesus is closed before it starts. If it isn't, the question stays open.</p>
<h3>How Jesus treated people who doubted</h3>
<p>John the Baptist, in prison, sent this question to Jesus:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>And when John had heard in prison about the works of Christ, he sent two of his disciples</p>
<p>and said to Him, “Are You the Coming One, or do we look for another?”</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2011%3A2-3&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 11:2–3</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>That is a real question from a man who was not sure.</p>
<p>This is how Jesus answered:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>Jesus answered and said to them, <span class="wj">“Go and tell John the things which you hear and see:</span></p>
<p><span class="wj"><em>The</em> blind see and <em>the</em> lame walk; <em>the</em> lepers are cleansed and <em>the</em> deaf hear; <em>the</em> dead are raised up and <em>the</em> poor have the gospel preached to them.</span></p>
<p><span class="wj">And blessed is he who is not offended because of Me.”</span></p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2011%3A4-6&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 11:4–6</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>And as John's disciples left, Jesus turned to the crowd and spoke about John:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>As they departed, Jesus began to say to the multitudes concerning John: <span class="wj">“What did you go out into the wilderness to see? A reed shaken by the wind?</span></p>
<p>…<span class="wj">“Assuredly, I say to you, among those born of women there has not risen one greater than John the Baptist; but he who is least in the kingdom of heaven is greater than he.</span></p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2011%3A7%2C%2011&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 11:7, 11</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Jesus answered John's doubt with evidence: what was being seen and heard, the blind seeing, the lame walking, the lepers cleansed and the deaf hearing. He added a gentle word, "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">blessed is he who is not offended because of Me</span></span>", which reads to me as an invitation and not a rebuke. Then, in front of everyone, He said that no one born of women had risen greater than John the Baptist. The doubt did not cancel John's standing.</p>
<p>Thomas is the clearest case. Jesus did not turn him away:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>Then He said to Thomas, <span class="wj">“Reach your finger here, and look at My hands; and reach your hand <em>here,</em> and put <em>it</em> into My side. Do not be unbelieving, but believing.”</span></p>
<p>And Thomas answered and said to Him, “My Lord and my God!”</p>
<p>Jesus said to him, <span class="wj">“Thomas, because you have seen Me, you have believed. Blessed <em>are</em> those who have not seen and <em>yet</em> have believed.”</span></p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020%3A27-29&amp;version=NKJV">John 20:27–29</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Jesus met Thomas's doubt by offering His hands and His side, and Thomas answered "<span class="sq">My Lord and my God!</span>" Jesus then pronounced a blessing on "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">those who have not seen and <em>yet</em> have believed</span></span>". I read that as a gentle lesson, not a rejection of Thomas.</p>
<p>Peter shows the same thing. When he began to sink, Jesus called him "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">of little faith</span></span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2014%3A31&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 14:31</a>). That phrase means he has some.</p>
<h3>The verse that seems to say yes</h3>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>But he who doubts is condemned if he eats, because <em>he does</em> not <em>eat</em> from faith; for whatever <em>is</em> not from faith is sin.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2014%3A23&amp;version=NKJV">Romans 14:23</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>This is the strongest verse for "doubt is sin", so it deserves a fair hearing. "<span class="sq">Doubts</span>" here is <span lang="el">διακρίνω</span> (<em>diakrinō</em>, G1252), the same word James uses for doubting.</p>
<p>As I read it, the sin the verse names is eating while doubting, "<span class="sq">because he does not eat from faith</span>". That is acting against your own conscience. It is not having a question.</p>
<p>The mainstream Christian tradition draws the same line. It separates doubt that is chosen or deliberately cultivated from doubt that is a struggle, and it holds that a believer's faith can be weak, assailed and shaken, and still be real faith that wins through. The sources are listed at the end.</p>
<p>So here is my answer. No, doubt on its own is not sin. What matters is what you do with it: bring it to God, or walk away. And that reopens the first question.</p>
</section>
<section id="human">
<h2>Fully God, and fully human</h2>
<p>I believe Jesus is fully God. Scripture is just as clear that He was fully human, and that He lived with human weakness.</p>
<p>After fasting forty days and forty nights, "<span class="sq">He was hungry</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%204%3A2&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 4:2</a>). Coming to Jacob's well, He was "<span class="sq">wearied</span>" from His journey (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%204%3A6&amp;version=NKJV">John 4:6</a>). At the grave of His friend Lazarus, "<span class="sq">Jesus wept</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2011%3A35&amp;version=NKJV">John 11:35</a>). On the cross He said, "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">I thirst!</span></span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2019%3A28&amp;version=NKJV">John 19:28</a>). And Paul says that "<span class="sq">He was crucified in weakness</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Corinthians%2013%3A4&amp;version=NKJV">2 Corinthians 13:4</a>).</p>
<p>He also said there was something He did not know:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p><span class="wj">“But of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father.</span></p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2013%3A32&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 13:32</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Hebrews says why He had to be like this:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>Therefore, in all things He had to be made like <em>His</em> brethren, that He might be a merciful and faithful High Priest in things <em>pertaining</em> to God, to make propitiation for the sins of the people.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%202%3A17&amp;version=NKJV">Hebrews 2:17</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>So when I ask whether Jesus doubted, I am not asking whether He stopped being God. I am asking whether doubt belongs with hunger, tiredness, grief and not knowing, among the human weaknesses He took on. I think it does.</p>
<p>And He did not give in to any of them. He felt every one of these weaknesses and He overcame them. He told His disciples, "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">be of good cheer, I have overcome the world</span></span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2016%3A33&amp;version=NKJV">John 16:33</a>). On the cross He doubted, and He overcame that too. He never let go of His Father.</p>
</section>
<section id="cross">
<h2>The cry from the cross: what David said, and what David felt</h2>
<p>"<span class="sq">Eli, Eli, lama sabachthani</span>" are the opening words of Psalm 22. Its title reads "<span class="sq">A Psalm of David.</span>"</p>
<h3>What David said</h3>
<p>I do believe Jesus was pointing to what David said. Psalm 22 reads like the cross. The NKJV capitalizes "Me" and "My" in this psalm, reading it as speaking of the Messiah. Look at verses 16 and 18:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>For dogs have surrounded Me;<br>The congregation of the wicked has enclosed Me.<br>They pierced My hands and My feet;</p>
<p>They divide My garments among them,<br>And for My clothing they cast lots.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A16%2C%2018&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:16, 18</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Hands and feet pierced, clothes divided by lot. Those are the words of someone surrounded and stripped of everything, and they read to me like the cross.</p>
<h3>What David felt</h3>
<p>But I don't think He was only pointing. If He took David's words, I think we have to look at what David was feeling when he wrote them.</p>
<p>Scripture doesn't tell us what was happening in David's life when he wrote this psalm, so I let the psalm speak for itself. It opens with the feeling of being forsaken, and of crying out and not being heard:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>My God, My God, why have You forsaken Me?<br><em>Why are You so</em> far from helping Me,<br><em>And from</em> the words of My groaning?</p>
<p>O My God, I cry in the daytime, but You do not hear;<br>And in the night season, and am not silent.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A1-2&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:1–2</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Forsaken, far from help, crying day and night and not heard. That is what David says he felt.</p>
<p>Then the shame and the loneliness:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>But I <em>am</em> a worm, and no man;<br>A reproach of men, and despised by the people.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A6&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:6</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>He says he has lost his standing even among the people around him.</p>
<p>But he is still holding on. The first words of the psalm are "<span class="sq">My God, My God</span>", and verse 3 goes straight on: "<span class="sq">But You <em>are</em> holy</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A3&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:3</a>). And in the middle of it he still asks for help:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>But You, O Lord, do not be far from Me;<br>O My Strength, hasten to help Me!</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A19&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:19</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>A man who had given up would not ask.</p>
<p>Then the turn:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>For He has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted;<br>Nor has He hidden His face from Him;<br>But when He cried to Him, He heard.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A24&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:24</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>This is how I read it. David felt forsaken, said so out loud to God, and never stopped calling Him "<span class="sq">My God</span>". That is doubt that has not become unbelief, the same shape as <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A24&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 9:24</a>.</p>
<p>When Jesus took David's words, I believe He took David's feelings with them: forsaken, crying and not answered, and still holding on to "<span class="sq">My God</span>".</p>
<h3>What commentators say</h3>
<p>These older commentators quoted the King James Version, so their wording differs.</p>
<p>On what was really happening, the Pulpit Commentary says:</p>
<blockquote class="confession">
<p>it arose from some incalculable affliction of soul. He was bearing the sins of the whole world; the Lord had laid on him the iniquity of us all; there was no one to comfort him in his heaviness; and the light of God's countenance was for the time withdrawn from him.</p>
<p>Yet there is no despair in this lamentable outcry.</p>
<footer><cite>Pulpit Commentary on Matthew 27:46 (BibleHub)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Ellicott's commentary puts it in one line:</p>
<blockquote class="confession">
<p>He tasted of despair as others had tasted, but in the very act of tasting, the words “My God” were as a protest against it, and by them He was delivered from it.</p>
<footer><cite>Ellicott's Commentary for English Readers on Matthew 27:46 (BibleHub)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>That fits my David point closely: the words "<span class="sq">My God</span>" are the thing that holds on.</p>
<p>Not everyone reads it this way. Meyer's commentary is the opposing view, and I want to quote it fairly:</p>
<blockquote class="confession">
<p>this subjective feeling must not be confounded with actual objective desertion on the part of God</p>
<footer><cite>Meyer's NT Commentary on Matthew 27:46 (BibleHub)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Serious readers disagree here, and I'm not claiming the matter is closed.</p>
<h3>The last words</h3>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>And when Jesus had cried out with a loud voice, He said, <span class="wj">“Father, ‘into Your hands I commit My spirit.’ ”</span> Having said this, He breathed His last.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2023%3A46&amp;version=NKJV">Luke 23:46</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>The last word from the cross is trust.</p>
</section>
<section id="gethsemane">
<h2>Gethsemane: the fear before the cross</h2>
<p>Gethsemane is supporting evidence, not the main event. It shows the fear was already there before the cross. The cross is where it was spoken.</p>
<p>Mark describes Jesus in the garden as "<span class="sq">troubled</span>" and "<span class="sq">deeply distressed</span>" in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2014%3A33&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 14:33</a>: "<span class="sq">He began to be troubled and deeply distressed.</span>" <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A37&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 26:37</a> also says "<span class="sq">He began to be sorrowful and deeply distressed.</span>" The Greek for "troubled" glosses as "to be greatly amazed, to be astounded, to be alarmed" (see the <a href="#words">word list</a>).</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>And being in agony, He prayed more earnestly. Then His sweat became like great drops of blood falling down to the ground.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A44&amp;version=NKJV">Luke 22:44</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>Some, though by no means all, of the oldest manuscripts omit <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A43-44&amp;version=NKJV">Luke 22:43–44</a>, so I don't rest the argument on this verse alone. <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2014%3A33&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 14:33</a> and <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A37&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 26:37</a> carry the same picture without it.</p>
<p>In the garden He prayed:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>…<span class="wj">“O My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass from Me; nevertheless, not as I will, but as You <em>will.</em>”</span></p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A39&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 26:39</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>He asks for the cup to pass, and He still walks forward.</p>
<p>I do not think it was the physical pain that Jesus feared. He had been telling His disciples that He would rise again, and He had spoken of the dead as sleeping, as with Lazarus. I think what He dreaded was the separation from God. Knowing how offensive sin is to God, He feared that their separation would be eternal.</p>

<p>This is my answer to the question I started with. Jesus was not afraid of what His followers faced. They faced death. He faced separation from His Father.</p>
</section>
<section id="rise">
<h2>But He said He would rise again</h2>
<p>That raises an objection, and I want to state it fairly. Jesus told His disciples what was coming:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>From that time Jesus began to show to His disciples that He must go to Jerusalem, and suffer many things from the elders and chief priests and scribes, and be killed, and be raised the third day.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2016%3A21&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 16:21</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>He also spoke of death as sleep. Before going to Lazarus He told the disciples, "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">Our friend Lazarus sleeps</span></span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2011%3A11&amp;version=NKJV">John 11:11</a>). So how could He fear that the separation from His Father would be eternal?</p>
<p>I don't think I can explain it fully, but here is how I see it. Believing a promise beforehand is one thing. Feeling it in the dark is another. Scripture shows both in Jesus, and it doesn't explain the mechanics.</p>
<p>The evidence that the certainty was not felt on the cross is His own word "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">why</span></span>" in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2027%3A46&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 27:46</a>. I know that a question does not always mean the one asking lacks the answer. Jesus once asked Philip where they could buy bread for a crowd, and John adds: "<span class="sq">But this He said to test him, for He Himself knew what He would do.</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%206%3A6&amp;version=NKJV">John 6:6</a>). When Jesus asked a question while knowing the answer, John tells us so. No Gospel writer says that about the cry from the cross. That is an argument from silence, so I hold it lightly.</p>
<p>What weighs more with me is everything around the question. In the garden He was "<span class="sq">troubled and deeply distressed</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2014%3A33&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 14:33</a>). On the cross He said He was forsaken. And the psalm He quoted goes straight on: "<span class="sq">I cry in the daytime, but You do not hear</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A2&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:2</a>). In that setting I read His why as a real one.</p>
<p>David does the same in Psalm 22. He trusts God, and he still cries out that God does not hear.</p>
<p>I don't think the text asks me to choose between them.</p>
</section>
<section id="lament">
<h2>Why I call it doubt, not lament</h2>
<p>Lament is the form of the prayer: raw grief and questions brought to God while still calling Him "<span class="sq">my God</span>".
</p>
<p>Doubt is what is inside this particular lament: not knowing, asking why, not being answered. Psalm 22 is a lament that carries doubt.</p>
<p>I should be honest that many readers use a narrower meaning of doubt, suspecting that God might not be faithful. On that meaning the answer is no, and nothing in the text shows Jesus suspecting that. On the meaning I set out at the start, wavering while still moving toward God, the answer is yes.</p>
</section>
<section id="mainstream">
<h2>Where I part with mainstream Christianity</h2>
<p>I want to be clear about where I agree with mainstream Christianity and where I don't.</p>
<p>We agree on a lot. Jesus is fully God and fully human. Doubt on its own is not sin. The cry from the cross is the first line of Psalm 22. And Jesus never stopped trusting His Father.</p>
<p>I part with it in two places.</p>
<p><strong>First, mainstream Christianity says the cry was not doubt.</strong> The usual teaching is that Jesus may have felt abandoned, but that He was quoting Psalm 22 in confidence and was not doubting God, since He still called Him "My God". I think that keeps the word doubt further from Jesus than the text does. He felt forsaken, He was not answered, and He asked why. On the meaning of doubt I set out at the start, that is doubt. I agree that it never became unbelief.</p>
<p><strong>Second, mainstream Christianity says He knew the separation was only temporary.</strong> The usual teaching is that Jesus knew His Father would not hide His face forever, and it points to the end of Psalm 22 (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A24&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 22:24</a>) and to <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%2012%3A2&amp;version=NKJV">Hebrews 12:2</a>, which says that He endured the cross "<span class="sq">for the joy that was set before Him</span>". I accept both verses. But I don't think they tell us what He could see in the dark at the ninth hour. A question does not always mean doubt, as I said above, but I read this one, asked in that darkness, as a real why. I think that on the cross He could not see past the separation, and that He feared it would be eternal.</p>
<p>So this is where I stand apart: mainstream Christianity says He felt forsaken but did not doubt. I say He doubted and did not disbelieve. The sources for the mainstream view are listed at the end, so you can read them in their own words.</p>
</section>
<section id="logic">
<h2>The argument in short</h2>
<ol class="steps">
<li>Doubt and faith can exist together (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A24&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 9:24</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2014%3A31&amp;version=NKJV">Matt 14:31</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2028%3A17&amp;version=NKJV">Matt 28:17</a>).</li>
<li>Doubt on its own is not sin (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=John%2020%3A27-29&amp;version=NKJV">John 20:27–29</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%2014%3A23&amp;version=NKJV">Rom 14:23</a> read in context).</li>
<li>Jesus was without sin (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3A15&amp;version=NKJV">Heb 4:15</a>), so asking whether He doubted is not asking whether He sinned.</li>
<li>He feared in Gethsemane (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%2014%3A33&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 14:33</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2022%3A44&amp;version=NKJV">Luke 22:44</a>) and still went forward (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A39&amp;version=NKJV">Matt 26:39</a>).</li>
<li>On the cross He asked why, in David's words, feeling what David felt (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2027%3A46&amp;version=NKJV">Matt 27:46</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2022%3A1-2&amp;version=NKJV">Ps 22:1–2</a>).</li>
<li>He never let go: "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">My God</span></span>", and "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">into Your hands</span></span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Luke%2023%3A46&amp;version=NKJV">Luke 23:46</a>).</li>
<li>That is doubt without unbelief.</li>
</ol>
<p>This is my conclusion, offered carefully, not settled doctrine.</p>
</section>
<section id="conclusion">
<h2>Conclusion</h2>
<p>So, did Jesus ever doubt? Yes, on the cross, but He never fell into unbelief.</p>
<p>Reading the cross and Gethsemane this way makes the sacrifice cost more, at least to me. Jesus wasn't enduring a painful weekend knowing everything would be fine on Monday. I think He was stepping into the darkness of separation from His Father, afraid He might not come back, and going anyway out of love and obedience.</p>
<p>This is what the study gave me, and it answered the question that had held my faith back. I no longer think Jesus was afraid of dying, or of the pain. He was afraid of being separated from His Father. That is something Stephen never had to face. Stephen died seeing the heavens opened (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Acts%207%3A56&amp;version=NKJV">Acts 7:56</a>). Jesus died asking why He had been forsaken.</p>
<p>It also changed how I see sin. If separation from God is what made Jesus afraid, then it is what I ought to be afraid of too. And sin is what separates us from God:</p>
<blockquote class="scripture">
<p>But your iniquities have separated you from your God;<br>
And your sins have hidden <em>His</em> face from you,<br>
So that He will not hear.</p>
<footer><cite><a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Isaiah%2059%3A2&amp;version=NKJV">Isaiah 59:2</a> (NKJV)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>I used to wonder why His death was such a big deal. I was looking at the nails. He was looking at the separation.</p>
<p>If doubt on its own isn't sin, saying this takes nothing from His sinlessness. The writer of Hebrews says He can "<span class="sq">sympathize with our weaknesses</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%204%3A15&amp;version=NKJV">Hebrews 4:15</a>).</p>
<p>If you are doubting right now, this is what I'd say. Doubt isn't sin. Bring it to God as the father did in <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A24&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 9:24</a>. Jesus did not lecture him. In the next three verses (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%209%3A25-27&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 9:25–27</a>) He healed his son.</p>
</section>
<section id="egw">
<h2>What Ellen G. White says on the issue</h2>
<p>That is where the study itself lands. Ellen G. White wrote on these same questions, so here is what she says. I give the reference codes so you can check each passage in context.</p>
<p>Ellen White quoted the King James Version, so the scripture wording inside her quotes differs.</p>
<h3>On the cross</h3>
<p>On the Father's face being hidden from Jesus on the cross:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>But now with the terrible weight of guilt He bears, He cannot see the Father's reconciling face. The withdrawal of the divine countenance from the Saviour in this hour of supreme anguish pierced His heart with a sorrow that can never be fully understood by man. So great was this agony that His physical pain was hardly felt.</p>
<footer><cite>DA 753.1 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 78, "Calvary")</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>On what He feared as the darkness closed in:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>Satan with his fierce temptations wrung the heart of Jesus. The Saviour could not see through the portals of the tomb. Hope did not present to Him His coming forth from the grave a conqueror, or tell Him of the Father's acceptance of the sacrifice. He feared that sin was so offensive to God that Their separation was to be eternal. Christ felt the anguish which the sinner will feel when mercy shall no longer plead for the guilty race. It was the sense of sin, bringing the Father's wrath upon Him as man's substitute, that made the cup He drank so bitter, and broke the heart of the Son of God.</p>
<footer><cite>DA 753.2 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 78, "Calvary")</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>On how it ended, with faith:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>Amid the awful darkness, apparently forsaken of God, Christ had drained the last dregs in the cup of human woe. In those dreadful hours He had relied upon the evidence of His Father’s acceptance heretofore given Him. He was acquainted with the character of His Father; He understood His justice, His mercy, and His great love. By faith He rested in Him whom it had ever been His joy to obey. And as in submission He committed Himself to God, the sense of the loss of His Father’s favor was withdrawn. By faith, Christ was victor.</p>
<footer><cite>DA 756.3 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 78, "Calvary")</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<h3>On Gethsemane</h3>
<p>On the fear in the garden, and where it came from:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>With the issues of the conflict before Him, Christ's soul was filled with dread of separation from God. Satan told Him that if He became the surety for a sinful world, the separation would be eternal. He would be identified with Satan's kingdom, and would nevermore be one with God.</p>
<footer><cite>DA 686.5 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 74, "Gethsemane")</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>To be accurate, here it is Satan who tells Jesus the separation would be eternal.</p>
<h3>On doubt</h3>
<p>On evidence and faith:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>God never asks us to believe, without giving sufficient evidence upon which to base our faith. [...] Yet God has never removed the possibility of doubt. Our faith must rest upon evidence, not demonstration.</p>
<footer><cite>SC 105.2 (Steps to Christ, ch. 12, "What to Do with Doubt")</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>On how Jesus treated Thomas:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>Jesus did not overwhelm Thomas with reproach, nor did He enter into controversy with him. He revealed Himself to the doubting one.</p>
<p>Unbelief is seldom overcome by controversy.</p>
<footer><cite>DA 808.1 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 84)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>And on what happens to those who wait for every doubt to be removed:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>Many who, like Thomas, wait for all cause of doubt to be removed, will never realize their desire. They gradually become confirmed in unbelief.</p>
<footer><cite>DA 807.5 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 84)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
<p>This one is the warning side. Doubt that waits for every question to be answered before moving toward God can harden.</p>
<p>I want to close with this one:</p>
<blockquote class="egw">
<p>Jesus is ever ready to speak peace to souls that are burdened with doubts and fears</p>
<footer><cite>DA 803.3 (The Desire of Ages, ch. 84)</cite></footer>
</blockquote>
</section>
<section id="words">
<h2>Word list</h2>
<p>These are the working definitions I used besides doubt and unbelief, followed by the words as the lexicons give them.</p>
<dl>
<dt>Little faith</dt>
<dd>Faith that is real but weak under pressure. It is Jesus' word for Peter sinking (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2014%3A31&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 14:31</a>). Jesus says both words to Peter in one breath: "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">of little faith</span></span>" is <span lang="el">ὀλιγόπιστε</span> (<em>oligopistos</em>, G3640) and "<span class="sq"><span class="wj">did you doubt</span></span>" is <span lang="el">ἐδίστασας</span> (<em>distazō</em>, G1365).</dd>
<dt>Double-minded</dt>
<dd>Divided between two directions (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=James%201%3A8&amp;version=NKJV">James 1:8</a>; see also <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=James%201%3A6&amp;version=NKJV">James 1:6</a>).</dd>
<dt>Fear, in two kinds</dt>
<dd>
<ul>
<li>Cowardice: running from what is right to save yourself (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=2%20Timothy%201%3A7&amp;version=NKJV">2 Timothy 1:7</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%208%3A26&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 8:26</a>; <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Mark%204%3A40&amp;version=NKJV">Mark 4:40</a>).</li>
<li>Dread: intense fear in the face of suffering while still walking forward. Jesus asks for the cup to pass, and still walks forward (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew%2026%3A39&amp;version=NKJV">Matthew 26:39</a>).</li>
</ul>
</dd>
<dt>Faith</dt>
<dd>Trust; being persuaded of something and placing confidence in it (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Hebrews%2011%3A1&amp;version=NKJV">Hebrews 11:1</a>). In <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%204%3A20&amp;version=NKJV">Romans 4:20</a>, "<span class="sq">waver</span>" is <span lang="el">διεκρίθη</span> (<em>diakrinō</em>, G1252) and "<span class="sq">unbelief</span>" is <span lang="el">ἀπιστίᾳ</span> (<em>apistia</em>, G570), so the doubt word and the unbelief word stand side by side about Abraham, and he came out "<span class="sq">strengthened in faith</span>" (<a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Romans%204%3A20-21&amp;version=NKJV">Romans 4:20–21</a>).</dd>
<dt>Lament</dt>
<dd>Bringing raw grief and questions directly to God while still calling Him "<span class="sq">my God</span>". <a href="https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalm%2013%3A1&amp;version=NKJV">Psalm 13:1</a> is one, and like Psalm 22, its title reads "<span class="sq">A Psalm of David.</span>"</dd>
</dl>
